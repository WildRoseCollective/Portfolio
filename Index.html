About

Who We Are

"We work collaboratively at the intersection of epistemic frames, producing deeply relational, feminist, environmental research-creation projects that are cross-disciplinary, cross-cultural, and cross-historical."

The Wildrose Collective is an award-winning team of humanities scholars, social scientists, and scientists united by shared urgency: the polycrisis of the Anthropocene demands forms of knowing and making that exceed any single discipline. Our methodology is sited, grounded, and feminist — attending to the particular places, histories, and bodies that environmental crisis presses upon. We fuse poetics and physics, botany and data science, citizen science and critical theory, always in service of more just relations with the living world.

Supported by the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada, our work spans VR installations, sound pieces, archival research, speculative fiction, interactive digital media, and peer-reviewed scholarship. We are committed to training the next generation of environmental humanities researchers while building durable public partnerships.


Affiliated institutions include Toronto Metropolitan University, Athabasca University, Ontario Tech University, University of Waterloo, York University, and the Canadian Research Knowledge Network.

Principal Investigator

Monique Tschofen is an interdisciplinary scholar whose work spans Canadian literature and culture, feminist and queer theory, environmental humanities, and digital media. She has developed award-winning research-creation projects at the intersection of technology, ecology, and art. Her current work explores speculative and posthumanist approaches to the environmental archive, and she leads the Wildrose Collective's methodological innovations in feminist ecological research-creation.

Projects

Research-Creation

Our projects take form as immersive installations, documentary films, sound compositions, digital archives, and experimental publications — always grounded in specific ecologies and communities, always attentive to the felt experience of environmental change.

Sound Installation · 2024

Listening to Wildfire

An acoustic ecology project that records and recomposes soundscapes from post-wildfire boreal zones in Northern Alberta, asking what fire sounds like from within, and what we can hear that data alone cannot tell us.

VR Installation
VR Installation · 2023–2024

Memory Eternal

A virtual reality installation staging intimate encounters with endangered plant species — guests inhabit the perspective of a wildrose, experiencing time at the scale of seasons, witnessing the slow grief of habitat loss from the inside.

Virtual reality Plant perspectives Phenomenology
Digital Humanities · Ongoing

Careless Water

An AI-driven deformance and research-creation project centred on water ecologies. Machine learning models trained on historical water texts generate new poetic forms, which are then performed, mapped, and read against contemporary watershed data.

Computational poetics Water ecologies AI deformance

Citizen Science · 2022–2025

Wayside Registers

A citizen botany and archival research project bringing together amateur naturalists, Indigenous plant knowledge keepers, and historical herbarium collections to document the changing presence of wildflower species across the Canadian prairies over 150 years.

Featured Initiative

Decameron 2.0

A courtyard and garden storyworld collective in which participants gather — physically and virtually — to share stories of environmental grief, memory, and resilience during a time of compounding crisis. Drawing on Boccaccio's original gesture of storytelling as a form of communal survival, Decameron 2.0 reframes the pandemic, climate crisis, and ecological loss as occasions for new forms of collective witness.


An ongoing public engagement initiative · 2021–present

From the Project

"Storytelling as Ecological Practice"

Each gathering produces new textual, sonic, and visual artifacts that are archived in a living Scalar publication. The project asks: what do we owe one another in the telling? What forms of attention does crisis demand? How does the garden hold the stories we cannot say elsewhere?
